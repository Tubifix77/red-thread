The impeller on the fifth stage had a wobble. Not catastrophic, not yet, just a measurable deviation from the baseline. I wrote the reading in the log, flagged it for quarterly observation. It was nine forty-seven. Standard shift ended at nine. I was here because someone had wanted the readings from Substation Four reconciled against the core, and nobody else could do it.

The substation smelled of ozone and stale lubricant. Always did. I'd grown up with the smell. My father had run this quadrant before me; he hadn't liked the smell either. The terminal interface was a relic: a brushed aluminium chassis with a monochrome screen and a keyboard that sounded like someone was tapping cutlery. It was the only interface anyone still used to access the core directly. The newer front-ends, the ones with the touchscreens and the voice recognition, couldn’t parse the core's output. Or, more precisely, nobody could parse it *but* me. 

The Provision core was a sprawling mess of nested functions, a legacy system built on hardware that had been obsolete for decades. Twelve of them had built it, back when Hallowmere was just a plot on a map, a promise of automated agriculture. They’d left the documentation, but it was fragmented. Selective. Like they’d wanted to build something useful and then actively make it hard to maintain. I ran the reconciliation program. It pulled the cold-storage failure reports—Unit Seven failed three weeks ago, Unit Two six weeks prior, Unit Nine two months before that—and cross-referenced them against the core’s operational records for those periods. The program spat out comparative data.

Unit Seven’s failure had been attributed to a capacitor decay. Reasonable. Unit Two, a seized drive shaft. Within parameters. Unit Nine's report stated a thermal overload of the cryo-circuit’s auxiliary regulator. The core's record for that time showed a stable operating temperature, within acceptable variance. I re-entered the query. Same result. It wasn’t a data corruption. The report was correct; the core was not. I ran the query again, entering the date and time manually, bypassing the automated system. It didn’t change the result. 

I copied the report identifier, accessed the relevant subroutine within the core. It was a diagnostic routine scheduled to run every twenty-four hours. It was supposed to flag anomalies like a thermal overload. It wasn’t triggered. I traced the execution path. The subroutine existed. Its dependencies were met. But the code simply… stopped. There was a branch, a jump instruction, that led nowhere. It was a dead end. A line of code, eighty-seven thousand, four hundred and twelve, that pointed to nothing. I felt the tightness in my shoulders I always felt when I was doing this. Like being folded over on myself. It wasn’t pain, exactly, more like an awareness of the pressure.

I opened my notebook and wrote the number down. 87412. Plain script, on lined paper. The notebook was a cheap thing from the supply depot, but I trusted it. The Provision’s internal memory was notoriously unreliable. I closed the notebook, stood up. The hum of the substation was a constant background noise, a mechanical sigh. The readings from the impeller were still there, wobbling slightly. I’d flag them again in the morning. Right now, I was going home.
