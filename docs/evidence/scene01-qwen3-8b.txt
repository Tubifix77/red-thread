The pump had been running eleven minutes longer than the log said it had. Siv Alderman leaned against the bulkhead, fingers curled around the edge of her notebook, its pages dog-eared and smudged with ink from the grease on her palms. The substation’s air smelled of ozone and burnt transformer oil, a scent she’d learned to associate with the Provision’s slow decay. She didn’t like the pump house. The walls were too close, the lights too harsh, and the machinery hummed with a frequency that made her teeth ache. But the core’s terminal was here, bolted to the wall beneath a dust layer thick enough to hide a body. She didn’t mind the dust. It meant the grid hadn’t cleaned the air filters in a week, and that meant the Provision was still operational, which meant she hadn’t been fired yet.  

The cold-storage logs were stacked on the workbench, their edges curled from the humidity. She ran her thumb across the third failure’s entry, the ink smudged where her fingers had pressed too hard. The log said the reservoir at farm nine had tripped its pressure valve at 02:17, triggering a cascade that shut down the cold-loop’s third segment. The core’s record, though, wasn’t matching. The Provision’s code was supposed to route the failure to a secondary reservoir, but the core’s output showed the main loop had collapsed without rerouting. The discrepancy was three seconds, but that was enough to make her jaw tighten. She tapped the terminal’s keyboard, the keys worn smooth from years of use. The screen flared to life, casting her face in blue light. She didn’t look at it. She trusted the core’s output, not the log. The logs were written by people who didn’t know how to read the code.  

She scrolled through the core’s timestamped entries, her eyes skimming the lines of hexadecimal and assembly. The failure at farm nine should have triggered a branch labeled *C-27*, a directive to divert cold flow to the secondary reservoir. But the core’s record showed the system had skipped *C-27* entirely, falling into a fallback routine that had been deprecated ten years ago. She frowned. Maybe she’d misread the log. Maybe the failure had been misclassified. She toggled the terminal’s interface, bringing up the log’s raw data. The entry was the same. The core’s output was the same. She exhaled, slow and measured, and turned to the terminal’s diagnostic panel. The Provision’s code was a labyrinth of nested loops and conditional branches, but she’d memorized the pathways. This wasn’t a glitch. This was a dead end.  

She pulled her notebook from her pocket, the plastic clip catching on the edge of the workbench. The pages were filled with line numbers and timestamps, a patchwork of data she’d compiled over the years. She traced her finger down the entries, stopping at the last line she’d added the night before. *C-27* was listed there, marked *unreachable*. She’d noted it as a curiosity, a line of code that never executed. The founders had written it, but the Provision’s runtime had never triggered it. She didn’t know why. She didn’t need to. Her job was to keep the system running, not to question its logic. Still, she’d recorded it. The Provision’s code was a relic, a closed system built by people who’d left no explanation. The founders had intended it to be self-sustaining, but the grid was failing. The cold-storage failures were the first sign. She’d seen the numbers. Three in two years. Each time, the engineers called it component age. She knew better. The Provision wasn’t aging. It was being made to fail.  

She closed the notebook, tucking it into her back pocket. The terminal’s screen flickered as she disconnected it, the cable snaking through her fingers like a live wire. The pump’s hum had softened, its rhythm slowing as the system recalibrated. She didn’t like the way the Provision’s machinery groaned. It sounded like a warning. But she didn’t have time for warnings. The court was nine days away, and she’d counted it out on the calendar twice, the way you check a number you already believe. There was no appeal. No outside authority. The founders had buried their covenants in the code, and the townspeople had accepted it as law. She wasn’t supposed to question it. She was just supposed to keep the lights on.  

She slipped out of the pump house, the cold night air biting her face. The sky was a bruised gray, the kind that promised rain but never delivered. She didn’t mind. The Provision’s grid had enough redundancy to weather a storm. The real danger was the code, the silent decay she could never prove. She walked past the storage tanks, their surfaces polished by years of condensation, and paused at the edge of the field. The land was dry, the soil cracked in places, but the Provision’s irrigation system had kept it from dying. She didn’t know if that was a good thing. Maybe the grid was keeping the town alive longer than it should. Maybe it was already too late.  

She didn’t think about that. She didn’t have time. She reached the road, her notebook tucked between her fingers, and started walking home. The line number *C-27* was written in the margins, a note to herself. She’d marked it with a star, the only symbol she used to highlight things that mattered. The Provision’s code was a trap, but she couldn’t prove it. Not yet. She’d wait until the next failure. Until the grid showed her more signs. Until the founders’ secrets bled into the system’s failure. For now, she’d keep the lights on. That was all she could do.
